Introduction
🧭

The Many Doors Into Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity is no longer a single elective bolted onto a computer lab — it's a career field students can enter through several distinct doors, starting well before graduation and, increasingly, before high school.

For students in grades 7 and 8, that path usually starts informally: career-awareness activities, interactive virtual environments, and low-barrier exploration tools that introduce cybersecurity roles long before a student ever declares a CTE sequence. This grant's own resource library — 143+ interactive EMATES activities, a 3D/2D Virtual Cybersecurity Career Exploration environment with 17 workforce avatars, and challenge-based "Easter egg" activities — was built to work at that early-exposure stage precisely because it requires no lab infrastructure and runs in any browser.

By grade 9, most participating states have translated that early exposure into a formal, state-coded Program of Study. Rather than a single mandated course, four broad pathways carry students from introduction to workforce- or college-ready credential:

💻

Computer Science Programs

Cybersecurity concepts embedded in CS curricula — secure coding, algorithms, and foundational security.

🌐

Information Technology Programs

Network security, system administration, infrastructure protection, and operational security management.

📈

Business Programs

Cybersecurity governance, risk management, compliance, policy development, and business continuity.

🛡️

Dedicated Cybersecurity Programs

Comprehensive, cybersecurity-focused curricula spanning technical, managerial, and operational competencies.

Layered on top of all four pathways are the mechanisms that shorten time-to-credential: dual credit and concurrent enrollment with a local community college, articulation agreements that guarantee transfer credit, and industry certifications (CompTIA Security+ and CySA+, Cisco CCST/CCNA, EC-Council) that give students a workforce-recognized credential before they ever set foot on a college campus. The rest of this site documents the model behind that structure, the evidence that it works, and ten state-level examples of it in practice.

The Replicable Model
🧩

Programs of Study (PoS) Model

The core deliverable of this initiative: a framework any state or district can use to define, code, and report a high school cybersecurity pathway — without requiring every state to adopt the same course code.

📐 Model Description

The Formal Framework for High School Cybersecurity Pathways of Study organizes cybersecurity content into the four pathways introduced above, then asks each state to map its own course system onto five common elements — a Primary Course Code, an Official Title, a Career Cluster, an Advanced/Capstone Code, and a Program of Study designation — rather than adopting a single national code. Illinois's own build-out of that mapping (Course 10011A001 / 10020A001, "Cybersecurity," under the Networking & Cybersecurity Pathway) served as the originating template that the project then helped nine additional states replicate inside their own state course systems (ISBE, IDOE, KSDE, MDE, DPI, DESE, NDE, ODEW, and Iowa's SCED-based system).

1. MapInventory the state's existing course codes and career-cluster taxonomy for anything cybersecurity-adjacent.
2. CodeEstablish (or confirm) a standalone Cybersecurity course code and, where possible, an advanced/capstone code.
3. ConnectAttach articulation and concurrent-enrollment agreements so the course carries college credit.
4. ReportRoute enrollment through the state's CTE reporting system so growth is visible to funders and policymakers.

The framework is explicitly aligned with:

NCAE-C designation requirements & knowledge units NIST/NICE Cybersecurity Workforce Framework State-specific CTE standards Industry certifications (CompTIA, (ISC)², GIAC) Academic transfer requirements

Pathway StandardizationThe first comprehensive model for standardizing high school cybersecurity pathways while preserving flexibility for state and institutional context — closing a gap the field had not previously addressed at scale.

Accelerated Program DevelopmentDocumented frameworks, lesson plans, and partnership models cut the time and resources a new institution needs to launch or enhance a program.
Faculty PreparationProfessional development and communities of practice measurably improved confidence among educators moving into cybersecurity instruction from other disciplines.

💡 Best Practices & Lessons Learned

Best practices identified through four years of state coordination:

Communicate early and oftenStrong, ongoing communication between high school and college faculty was the single strongest predictor of a durable concurrent-enrollment relationship.
Set expectations explicitlyClear articulation of course expectations and college-level rigor prevents credit disputes after a student transfers.
Invest in instructors, not just coursesOngoing professional development for concurrent-enrollment instructors sustains quality after the initial launch.
Build advising into the pathwayRobust student advising and support systems kept persistence rates high across partner states.

How real obstacles were solved:

ChallengeLimited number of certified cybersecurity instructors at the high school level
SolutionTeacher credentialing pathways plus remote lab access through the EPNC Virtual Lab initiative
ChallengeWide variation in state requirements for teaching cybersecurity courses
SolutionA flexible framework emphasizing shared principles over prescriptive, one-size-fits-all requirements
ChallengeEquipment and laboratory infrastructure needs for hands-on instruction
SolutionCloud-based virtual labs and browser-based EMATES activities that need no on-site hardware
ChallengeCoordination complexity across many institutions and states
SolutionNamed state coordinators, a hybrid virtual/in-person meeting cadence, and a train-the-trainer capacity model

Five lessons the project team would tell any state starting this work:

1
Coordination beats broadcast.

A named state coordinator who understands local approval processes, stakeholders, and politics outperforms any amount of centrally-produced guidance.

2
Face-to-face still matters.

In-person state meetings built relationship foundations that purely virtual engagement could not replicate, even though virtual options remained essential for reach.

3
Flexibility is not optional.

A one-size-fits-all course code or requirement does not survive contact with ten different state CTE systems — the framework has to guide, not dictate.

4
Turnkey resources move faster than guidance documents.

Institutions adopted ready-to-use lesson plans and templates far faster than they acted on advisory documentation alone.

5
Communities outlast champions.

Distributing leadership across a network of peers, rather than relying on one person per state, is what keeps a pathway alive when a champion changes roles.
